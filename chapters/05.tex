\title{Diachronic paths to uninflectedness in South Slavic}

\abstract{We argue that the ongoing loss of case in Serbian and Bulgarian dialects has led to a split system with competing co-grammars: one where nouns inflect and another where they do not. This division between \textit{forerunners} (without inflection) and \textit{underachievers} (with inflection) results in partial rules of different kinds. There are three types of splits depending on the dialect. First, a split between morphological classes\is{morphological!class}, where some retain case distinctions while others do not. Second, classes that retain inflection may still exhibit uninflectedness in specific constructions, such as partitive constructions. Third, an otherwise inflecting morphological class\is{morphological!class} may split on a semantic basis, such as animate vs. inanimate. These asymmetries in morphological change lead to the rise of partial synchronic rules, whether morphological, syntactic, or semantic. These rules persist over time and evolve as historical processes move into new phases. By studying these rules in contemporary dialects and arranging them chronologically, we can uncover the detailed stages of this historical process.}

\section{Introduction} 
The loss of inflectional\is{inflection} case marking\is{case!marking} is an ongoing process in the dialect continuum\is{dialect!continuum} that stretches across \ili{Serbian} and \ili{Bulgarian} linguistic territory. This has led to substantial \isi{variation} in the \isi{dialect} case systems\is{case!system} of the region. We observe the first signs of this historical change in systems with rich \isi{case} distinctions, where case marking\is{case!marking} is obligatory on all classes of nominals\is{nominal}, as in the western part of the continuum\is{dialect!continuum}. Further east, we find dramatically reduced case paradigms\is{case!paradigm}, with just a few distinct \isi{case} forms, attested with only part of the lexicon, while the rest of the lexicon has lost the ability to inflect for \isi{case}. At the same time, in these dialects\is{dialect} we find competing rules of case marking\is{case!marking}, which appear to reflect different historical periods in the breakdown of the case system\is{case!system}. The result is either competition between more or less distinct \isi{inflection} (towards the west, in \ili{Serbian} dialects\is{dialect}), or competition between \isi{inflection} and uninflectedness (towards the east, in \ili{Bulgarian} dialects\is{dialect}). We investigate factors which underlie this competition and show how they vary at different stages of case decline\is{case!decline}. This provides us with an insight into the historical mechanisms underlying case loss\is{case!loss of case}, and to the conditions that may foster this change.

While our analysis has confirmed the relevance of the factors previously proposed to explain variable case marking\is{case!marking} synchronically\is{synchrony} and diachronically\is{diachrony} (e. g. \citealt{moravcsik1978, bossong1991, konig2008, dalrymplenikolaeva2011, cristofaro2013, kurumadajaeger2015, witzlackmakarevichserzant2018}), the data obtained from \ili{Serbian} and \ili{Bulgarian} dialects\is{dialect} shed new light on these factors and show that they do not in fact operate in the way that has previously been suggested.

First, extensive typological data show that explicit case marking\is{case!marking} is used in environments where it is essential to avoid ambiguity (e.g. between subjects\is{subject} and objects\is{object}) and is omitted where syntactic roles\is{syntactic!role} are transparent \citep{comrie1977}. For example, it has been shown that the omission of morphological case marking\is{case!marking} goes hand-in-hand with the use of adpositions\is{adposition}, which to a certain extent duplicate the information conveyed by \isi{case} \isi{inflection}. This has been shown to play a role in diachronic\is{diachrony} processes affecting case systems\is{case!system}: the information redundancy of \isi{case} \isi{inflection} in adpositional constructions\is{adpositional construction} may make these constructions more prone to the loss of morphological case\is{case!loss of case} than those without adpositions, where \isi{case} \isi{inflection} provides critical information about syntactic roles\is{syntactic!role} \citep{hewsonbubenik2006}. In the dialects\is{dialect} of East Serbia, while the use of adpositions\is{adposition} (prepositions\is{preposition}) correlates with a decline in case distinctions\is{case!decline}, the dialects\is{dialect} display a pattern which is the opposite of what is predicted by the above principle: the original case marking\is{case!marking} is better preserved precisely when governed by a \isi{preposition}, and more prone to loss elsewhere.

{\sloppy Second, pragmatic requirements, in particular \isi{discourse} salience, have been proven to be another important factor in variable case marking\is{case!marking} cross-linguistically. To mention just two examples, \citet{mcgregor2018} and \citet{montaut2018} present somewhat similar situations in unrelated languages (Southern African Khoisan\il{Khoisan!Southern African} and \ili{Dravidian} respectively) where the rise of \isi{object} case marking\is{case!marking} led to pragmatically motivated choices between overt case marking\is{case!marking} (\isi{accusative}) and zero marking: prominent objects\is{object} receive overt case marking\is{case!marking} while backgrounded objects\is{object} do not. In \ili{Bulgarian} border-region dialects\is{dialect}, where just two cases\is{case} have been preserved (\isi{nominative} and \isi{accusative}), explicit case marking\is{case!marking} on non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} nouns\is{noun} (\isi{accusative}) is also conditioned by pragmatic factors\is{pragmatic!factor}. However, it is not associated with pragmatic salience\is{pragmatic!salience}, but rather the opposite: explicit (\isi{accusative}) marking on non-subjects\is{subject!non-subject} is more frequent in pragmatically less prominent parts of an utterance (e.g. containing background information). Pragmatic prominence instead tends to trigger \isi{nominative} marking on non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} nouns\is{noun}, which makes them morphologically indistinguishable from subjects\is{subject}.\par}

We present two \isi{case} studies that show how different factors shape the transition from a larger to a smaller case system\is{case!system}, and further to uninflectedness. After the essential background information in Section \ref{05sec2}, we consider the role of structural factors, which are dominant in \ili{Serbian} dialects\is{dialect} (Section \ref{05sec3}), and analyse the impact of pragmatic conditions\is{pragmatic!condition}, which are dominant in the \ili{Bulgarian} dialects\is{dialect} under study (Section \ref{05sec4}). We suggest that this shift in prominence is due to the diachronic\is{diachrony} stage each variety has reached: structural factors are prominent at earlier stages of case decline\is{case!decline} and pragmatic factors\is{pragmatic!factor} at an advanced stage.

A group of dialects\is{dialect} in the west of the transitional area preserve all six original Proto-Slavic\il{Slavic!Proto-Slavic} cases\is{case} (plus vocative), but speakers regularly replace three of them with the \isi{accusative} form, which serves as a general oblique\is{oblique!general} \isi{case} (Figure \ref{05tab2}, Transitional System 1). The chronology that this transition reflects has been reconstructed primarily on the basis of liturgical texts produced within various regional written traditions in the South Slavic territory from the 10\textsuperscript{th} century onwards (\citealt{tsonev1984, duridanov1956, duridanov1958, rusek1964, cesko1970, belic1905}) and, for later periods, on the basis of clerical and household documents \citep{bernstejn1948}.

It is believed that the decay of the case system\is{case!system} originated in the South Slavic eastern zone (\ili{Bulgarian} and \ili{Macedonian}) around the 10\textsuperscript{th} century \citep[25]{duridanov1958}. By the end of the 14\textsuperscript{th} century morphological case marking\is{case!marking} on nominals\is{nominal} was completely lost in at least some of the dialects\is{dialect} spoken in the eastern zone (\ili{Bulgarian} border-region dialects\is{dialect}) \citep[11]{cesko1970}. Even earlier, by the end of the 13\textsuperscript{th} century, case loss\is{case!loss of case} was at an advanced stage in a number of \ili{Macedonian} dialects\is{dialect} \citep{rusek1964}. In \ili{Serbian}, the process of case decline\is{case!decline} started sometime before the 12\textsuperscript{th} century and affected the South-East dialects\is{dialect} \citep{belic1905}. It did not, however, lead to the complete loss of \isi{case} distinctions: rather it resulted in different types of reduced systems.


Historical evidence points to the following processes involved in this change: the spread of prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} replacing bare noun phrases\is{noun!phrase}; the redistribution of functions between indirect cases\is{case!indirect} and displacement of less productive indirect cases\is{case!indirect}; the spread of \isi{accusative} forms in the function of an oblique case\is{case!oblique} generalising across original indirect cases\is{case!indirect};\footnote{The chronology of the displacement of individual indirect cases\is{case!indirect} varies. The most stable, as dialectological\is{dialect} data of the \ili{Serbian} language show (\citealt{belic1905, miloradovic2003}) is the \isi{dative} \isi{case}. The historical data from \ili{Bulgarian} also testify to this: the \isi{dative} forms of nouns\is{noun} were found in \ili{Bulgarian} texts of the 17\textsuperscript{th}, 18\textsuperscript{th} and even 19\textsuperscript{th} centuries, while the \isi{genitive}, \isi{instrumental} and local cases\is{case!loss of case} had already fallen out of use in \ili{Bulgarian} dialects\is{dialect} in the 13\textsuperscript{th}-14\textsuperscript{th} centuries (\citealt[255--260]{mircev1958}; \citealt[301--310]{cesko1970}).} and the spread of uninflected forms replacing this oblique case\is{case!oblique}.

\section{Constructional \isi{variation}: the loss of the \isi{genitive} in \ili{Serbian} dialects\is{dialect}} \label{05sec3}

The loss of case\is{case!loss of case} in European languages has been reported to go hand in hand with the increased use of prepositions\is{preposition} (\citealt{blake2004, hewsonbubenik2006, kulikov2006}). \citet[178]{blake2004} illustrates this with an Old English\il{English!Old} version of Luke 15:15 (\ref{05ex3a}) and Wyclif's fourteenth-century translation (\ref{05ex3b}) (glossing and translation are as in \citealt{blake2004}):

In (\ref{05ex3a}), the directional (`to the citizen') and the \isi{possessive} (`of that country') relationships are expressed via \isi{inflection}, while in (\ref{05ex3b}), they are expressed by prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction}. The relationship between the two processes, however, is not clear. Did the spread of prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} cause case\is{case!loss of case} to disappear as it became semantically redundant (as claimed in \citealt{hjelmslev1935, hewsonbubenik2006})? Or was it, by contrast, a repair strategy necessary after the loss of explicit case\is{case!loss of case} marking, as proposed, for example, in \citet{skautrup1944}?

The \isi{variation} found in \ili{Serbian} dialects\is{dialect} allows us to investigate the interplay of inflectional\is{inflection} case marking\is{case!marking} and prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} using synchronic\is{synchrony} data. We focus here on the \isi{genitive}, which is in the process of being replaced by the \isi{accusative}. Specifically, we ask: do prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} particularly favour this replacement? This is what an \isi{informativeness} principle would predict: since the \isi{preposition} provides semantic context, \isi{genitive} case marking\is{case!marking} is redundant and the more general \isi{accusative} \isi{case} can be used without any loss of information (cf. \citealt{comrie1977, comrie1989}). Our data, however, show the opposite tendency: prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} favour the \emph{retention} of the \isi{genitive}, while its replacement by the \isi{accusative} is more advanced in non-prepositional contexts\is{prepositional!construction}. That means that in \ili{Serbian} dialects\is{dialect}, some principle other than \isi{informativeness} is at play. We suggest below that this principle could be understood as a speaker-oriented strategy of grammatical change.

In Section \ref{05sec3.1}, we introduce the data used in this \isi{case} study. Section \ref{05sec3.2} outlines the use of the \isi{genitive} forms in \ili{Serbian} and discusses the results of previous research on the \isi{variation} between \isi{genitive} and \isi{accusative} in South-East \ili{Serbian} dialects\is{dialect}. Sections \ref{05sec3.3} and \ref{05sec3.4} present the patterns of \isi{variation} found in our data; Section \ref{05sec3.5} discusses the theoretical implications of our findings.

\subsection{Data} \label{05sec3.1}

The data for this study come from sociolinguistic interviews with speakers of \ili{Serbian} dialects\is{dialect} located in or near Kosovo. These come from two sources: the archive of the Institute for Balkan Studies and our own fieldwork. The archive provided a subset of recordings collected in the project ``Research of Slavic Vernaculars in Kosovo and Metohija'' (2002 -- 2003) led by the Institute for the \ili{Serbian} Language of SASA and financed by UNESCO. Our data come from fieldwork in the municipality of Brus, Central Serbia, conducted by Maria Kyuseva within the project ``Declining case: inflectional\is{inflection} loss in progress''\is{case!decline} in 2022.

These data were collected in a uniform fashion that conforms to the \ili{Serbian} dialectological\is{dialect} tradition. The participants were native speakers of local dialects\is{dialect}, aged between 70 and 81 years, who had been long-term residents of the region and had engaged in traditional activities throughout their lives, such as cattle breeding or agriculture. This sampling ensured the minimal interference of the high-prestige standardised variety of \ili{Serbian}. The topics\is{topic} of the interviews included history, tradition, culture, crafts, cuisine, everyday life, and biographical stories.

The total corpus compiled from these two sources contains over 274,800 tokens, 30 hours and 36 minutes of recordings. Overall, twenty-three speakers from eleven villages were recorded. The Zeta-South Sandžak \isi{dialect} is marked in green, the Kosovo-Resava \isi{dialect} is marked in pink, and the Prizren-South Morava \isi{dialect} is marked in purple.

\subsection{Genitive \isi{case} in \ili{Serbian}} \label{05sec3.2}

The \ili{Serbian} genitive is highly polysemous. Its typical function is to mark a relationship between two entities, such as possessor-possessee, part-whole, and source-goal. Additionally, it can denote time, reason, goal, and manner. From the point of view of syntax, it has a wide distribution. It can be used in adnominal\is{adnominal construction}, adjectival\is{adjectival construction}, and verbal constructions\is{verbal construction}, as well as in prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} and constructions\is{construction} with a \isi{quantifier}. Out of all the cases\is{case}, the genitive occurs with the widest inventory of prepositions\is{preposition} \citep{felesko1955}.

Various studies of individual dialects\is{dialect} of South-East Serbia have already established the heterogeneous behaviour of the genitive in different constructions\is{construction}. For example, \citet{miloradovic2003} analyses the competition between the genitive and \isi{accusative} in the Kosovo-Resava \isi{dialect} of the Paraćin region in Central Serbia. She observes that while the genitive is dominant in prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction}, its use is highly limited elsewhere. In verbal constructions\is{verbal construction}, the genitive is replaced by the \isi{accusative} (\ref{05ex4a}), in adnominal constructions\is{adnominal construction} -- by a prepositional phrase\is{prepositional!phrase} (\ref{05ex4b}), and in adjectival constructions\is{adjectival construction}, it can be replaced either by the \isi{accusative} or by a prepositional phrase\is{prepositional!phrase} (\ref{05ex4c}).

\is{syntactic!factor|(}
\subsection{Discussion: structural factors in case decline\is{case!decline}} \label{05sec3.5}

These data illustrate both syntactic\is{syntactic!condition} and morphological conditions\is{morphological!condition} on \isi{variation} between the \isi{genitive} and \isi{accusative}. In terms of syntax, certain constructions\is{construction} favour the use of the \isi{accusative} over the \isi{genitive}, in particular prepositional phrases\is{prepositional!phrase}. In terms of morphology, certain inflection classes\is{inflectional class} are more likely to adopt the innovative \isi{accusative}, in particular class II.

The tendency of prepositional phrases\is{prepositional!phrase} to preserve the original case marking\is{case!marking} contradicts commonly held assumptions about the role played by prepositions\is{preposition} in case loss\is{case!loss of case} (\citealt{blake2004, hewsonbubenik2006}). Since \isi{genitive} case marking\is{case!marking} in these prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} is semantically redundant, it could be omitted without incurring any ambiguity. One might assume then that prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} would be the first to lose the \isi{genitive}. The fact that our data show the opposite means that the preservation of the \isi{genitive} is not driven by the need to disambiguate. We propose here a possible alternative motivation.

Following \citet{norde2002}, we distinguish two types of less-effort strategies for grammatical change: a speaker-oriented and a hearer-oriented strategy. These can be thought of as two opposing forces that shape any language change. The speaker ``wants'' to make less effort in encoding the message, and the hearer ``wants'' to make less effort in decoding the message. The \isi{informativeness} principle aligns with a hearer-oriented strategy. In this instance, the \isi{genitive} should first be replaced in prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction}, as it is not needed for the hearer to be able to correctly interpret the message, while it has a function in other constructions\is{construction}. А speaker-oriented strategy would be one that favours ease of production. The \isi{genitive} \isi{case} after the \isi{preposition} \emph{od} `from' is syntactically determined: \emph{od} governs the \isi{genitive}, creating a high degree of \isi{predictability}: every time the \isi{preposition} \emph{od} `from' is used, it can be expected to be followed by the \isi{genitive}. In adnominal constructions\is{adnominal construction} and constructions\is{construction} with a \isi{quantifier}, there is no such syntactic determination, in the sense that the nouns\is{noun}, such as `cup' or `edge', do not obligatorily govern a noun phrase\is{noun!phrase}, and quantifiers\is{quantifier} can similarly be used on their own. In this way, the syntactic information supports the \isi{case} morphology and contributes to the preservation of the original \isi{case} forms. Crucially, there is no gain for the hearer in preserving the \isi{case} forms in prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} while omitting them in non-prepositional constructions. Rather, it is in the speaker's interest to keep an inflected form in a context that always requires it.

With these results in mind, we turn to the contrasting patterns of \ili{Bulgarian} border-region dialects\is{dialect}\is{syntactic!factor|)}.

\subsection{Hypothesis: information structure conditions case selection\is{case!selection}}

Immediate observations made during the sociolinguistic interviews and further elicitation work led us to the following hypothesis. Variation\is{variation} between the \isi{nominative} and \isi{accusative} on non-subjects\is{subject!non-subject} is conditioned by information structure and pragmatic salience\is{pragmatic!salience}: nouns\is{noun} associated with new and pragmatically important information tend to lose specific non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} case marking\is{case!marking} and attract \isi{nominative}; nouns\is{noun} associated with background information and pragmatically less salient\is{pragmatic!salience} parts of an utterance tend to retain specific non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} marking and show stronger preference for the \isi{accusative}.

In what follows, we briefly consider the role of pragmatic factors\is{pragmatic!factor} in case selection\is{case!selection} within a general linguistic context (Section \ref{05sec4.3}) and then proceed to the analysis of our data (Section \ref{05sec4.4}) and a discussion (Section \ref{05sec4.5}). We argue that pragmatic conditions\is{pragmatic!condition} play a crucial role in speakers' choices between the two competing cases\is{case} which mark non-subjects\is{subject!non-subject} and in the decline of the \isi{accusative} as distinct morphological marking for non-subjects\is{subject!non-subject}. At the same time, we will show that the relationship between \isi{case} and information structure in \ili{Bulgarian} border-region dialects\is{dialect} differs significantly from that observed cross-linguistically, and offer an explanation for this.

\subsection{Information structure as a condition on case marking\is{case!marking}} \label{05sec4.3}

Information structure is claimed as a cause for variable case marking\is{case!marking} cross\hyp{}linguistically (e.g. \citealt{aikhenvald2010, iemmolo2010, valle2011, dalrymplenikolaeva2011}). First, a large body of typological research links differential object marking (DOM)\is{differential object marking} to \isi{disambiguation}: typical objects\is{object} which cannot be confused for subjects\is{subject} are less likely to receive specific grammatical marking than atypical ones, where such marking is essential for \isi{disambiguation} (\citealt{silverstein1976, comrie1977} \emph{inter alia}). With respect to information structure, this would mean that topical objects\is{object!topical} need to be marked to avoid ambiguity with subjects\is{subject}, while objects\is{object} occupying their typical slot as part of the pragmatic \isi{focus} may remain unmarked. In some languages specific non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} markers are used with highly topical objects\is{object!topical} dislocated\is{object!dislocated} from their default position (\citealt{iemmolo2010, aikhenvald2003}). Second, the degree of pragmatic prominence is another factor which may affect case marking\is{case!marking}: it has been shown for a number of DOM languages\is{differential object marking} that more prominent objects\is{object} with a higher pragmatic status are more likely to be marked for case\is{case!marking} than pragmatically less salient\is{pragmatic!salience} objects\is{object} (\citealt{aissen2003, malchukov2008}).

It is useful to assess the South Slavic situation against this broad cross-linguistic picture. The data from \ili{Bulgarian} border-region dialects\is{dialect} provide no evidence for case marking\is{case!marking} being used as a tool in contexts where atypical objects\is{object} (e.g. topicalised\is{object!topical} and dislocated\is{object!dislocated} to non-default positions or used with omitted governors\is{governor}) may be confused with subjects\is{subject} (see the analysis of the relationship between \isi{word order}, pragmatic structure\is{pragmatic!structure} and case marking\is{case!marking} in Section \ref{05sec4.4}). Thus, the situation in the border-region dialects\is{dialect} is different, in broad terms, to a situation in some of the DOM languages\is{differential object marking} (such as those considered in \citealt{aissen2003, malchukov2008}) where pragmatically more salient\is{pragmatic!salience} nouns\is{noun} attract case marking\is{case!marking} while less pragmatically salient\is{pragmatic!salience} nouns\is{noun} do not. In the \ili{Bulgarian} dialects\is{dialect} in question, case marking\is{case!marking} on non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} NPs\is{noun!phrase} is conditioned, to a significant extent, by whether they are associated with background or new information in an utterance, or with the \isi{topic}--\isi{focus} distinction. However, a significant peculiar feature of these dialects\is{dialect} is that specific non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} case marking\is{case!marking} (\isi{accusative}) is more frequent on nouns\is{noun} which are backgrounded. Nominative\is{nominative} forms (which may be used in \isi{subject} and non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} position, as examples in (\ref{05ex14}) and (\ref{05ex15}) demonstrate) are more frequent in the pragmatically salient\is{pragmatic!salience} part of an utterance. We will consider this in greater detail in the next section. It should be noted that in qualifying parts of an utterance as, \isi{topic} or \isi{focus} we follow the tradition of defining \isi{topic} as existing, background knowledge, or ``\isi{common ground}'', and \isi{focus} as part of an utterance which brings in new information and thus modifies the ``\isi{common ground}'' \citep{hoopswart2000, krifka2007}. Since originally our data collection was not intended to test the impact of information structure on case marking\is{case!marking}, and such analysis was applied later, we used broad definitions based on this opposition, and excluded from the analysis utterances where a non-ambiguous definition of information structure was not possible. Under this view, three types of utterances required special decisions: utterances with contrastive topic\is{topic!contrastive} as in (\ref{05ex16}), those with repeated focus\is{focus!repeated}, as in (\ref{05ex17}) and those with contrastive focus\is{focus!contrastive}, as in (\ref{05ex18}).

Following the approach taken in this study, we grouped together utterances where non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} nouns\is{noun} appear in the repeated focus\is{focus!repeated}, as in (\ref{05ex17}), and in the \isi{topic} on the one hand, and those where non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} nouns\is{noun} are part of the contrastive topic\is{topic!contrastive}, as in (\ref{05ex16}), and of the \isi{focus}. The rationale behind both decisions is based on information novelty. In the former case, information in the repeated focus\is{focus!repeated} is already brought into the \isi{discourse} and cannot be defined as changing the `\isi{common ground}'. In the latter case, contrastive topic\is{topic!contrastive} turns out to be very close to \isi{focus}, in that the contrast itself contributes to existing knowledge and thus modifies the `\isi{common ground}'. The similarity of \isi{focus} and contrastive topic\is{topic!contrastive} has been demonstrated in a number of studies within Alternative Semantics, where both concepts are understood as parts of an utterance which provide new information by singling out existing alternatives (\citealt[75--77]{rooth1992}; \citealt[64--68]{buring2016}). A small number of examples were annotated as contrastive focus\is{focus!contrastive}, as in (\ref{05ex18}). Contrastive focus\is{focus!contrastive} is not different from \isi{focus} in that it, like \isi{focus}, provides new information and modifies the `\isi{common ground}', under some specific conditions such as polarity of unexpectedness (e. g. \citealt[348]{zimmermann2008}; \citealt[117--126]{goodhue2022}). For this reason, in the analysis presented in Section \ref{05sec4.4} we did not distinguish between the two types of \isi{focus}. However, a possible effect of contrastive focus\is{focus!contrastive} will be discussed separately in the account presented in Section \ref{05sec4.5}.

We will consider the effect of information structure on non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} case marking\is{case!marking} with respect to two other factors: \isi{word order} and phrase\is{noun!phrase} type (NP\is{noun!phrase} vs. PP\is{prepositional!phrase}).

Previous research has suggested that the diachronic\is{diachrony} loss of case marking\is{case!marking} is accompanied by compensatory freezing of \isi{word order} as a means of distinguishing grammatical roles\is{grammatical role} \citep{blake2004}. Among other things, this implies that if an \isi{object} \isi{noun} occurs outside of its expected position, it will be more likely to preserve its original case marking\is{case!marking}. This straightforward complementarity between fixed \isi{word order} and case marking\is{case!marking} does not necessarily hold (cf. \citealt{allen2006} for Old English\il{English!Old} and \citealt{detges2009} for Old French\il{French!Old}); in fact, the opposite correlation may occur. Data from \ili{Japanese} show that \isi{word order} alternations may loosen grammatical relationships within a sentence so that dislocated objects\is{object!dislocated} may be \textit{less} likely to receive expected case marking\is{case!marking} than their counterparts in neutral positions within a basic constituent structure (cf. \ili{Japanese} scrambling of \isi{accusative}-marked objects\is{object} to a position where only the \isi{nominative} may be assigned, \citealt{kasai2018}).

The effect of phrase type (noun phrases\is{noun!phrase} vs. prepositional phrases\is{prepositional!phrase}) on case selection\is{case!selection} and decline of one of the competing cases\is{case} was surveyed in Section \ref{05sec3}. Here we will only reiterate the observation made above that the correlation between the use of prepositions\is{preposition} and the tendency to maintain an original \isi{case} form has been shown to be strong for the \isi{dialect} types under study. Therefore, we found it important to disentangle different conditions on case marking\is{case!marking} in order to assess their effect individually without interference from other conditions. To do so, we will analyse the effect of \isi{topic}-\isi{focus} distinction separately for different types of \isi{word order} and different phrase types (NP\is{noun!phrase} and PP\is{prepositional!phrase})\is{information structure|)}.

\subsection{Discussion: pragmatic factors\is{pragmatic!factor} in case decline} \label{05sec4.5}

The reported findings leave little doubt that \isi{information structure} has a crucial effect on \isi{variation} in case marking\is{case!marking} in \ili{Bulgarian} border-region dialects\is{dialect}. We hypothesise that the historical competition of case marking\is{case!marking} here is motivated by pragmatic salience\is{pragmatic!salience}. What we observe, however, is not a tight coupling between a pragmatic role and grammatical form, but rather a tendency for caseless\is{case} forms (historically the \isi{nominative}) to be generalised first across the pragmatically most salient\is{pragmatic!salience} positions, and only then to spread further to other parts of the utterance. \section{Conclusion}

The ongoing loss of case\is{case!loss of case} in \ili{Serbian} and \ili{Bulgarian} dialects\is{dialect} results in the competition between two forms, one of them more specific and one of them more general. How exactly this competition is manifested depends on the particular case system\is{case!system} it occurs in. In more conservative varieties that retain in principle a six-\isi{case} system (\ili{Serbian}), competition is between a more specific \isi{case} (e.g. the \isi{genitive}) and a general oblique\is{oblique!general} (\isi{accusative}). In more innovative varieties (\ili{Bulgarian}), where case loss\is{case!loss of case} is more advanced, the competition is between the general oblique\is{oblique!general} form (\isi{accusative}) and an uninflected stem. A particularly interesting result of our study has been to uncover key factors underlying these systems of competing case marking\is{case!marking}. Broadly construed, they match what has been observed cross-linguistically, but the principles that govern them are different. Elsewhere, prior work has shown that in case systems\is{case!system} which allow for more explicit and less explicit marking for a given syntactic role\is{syntactic!role}, more explicit case marking\is{case!marking} has two major functions: (i) indicating the syntactic role\is{syntactic!role} of nominals\is{nominal} in contexts where this is not otherwise obvious, and (ii) highlighting nominals\is{nominal} that are semantically or pragmatically prominent. In the \ili{Serbian} and \ili{Bulgarian} dialects\is{dialect} investigated here, the factors conditioning the competition of cases\is{case} appear to be the exact opposite.

First, contexts where the syntactic role\is{syntactic!role} of a \isi{nominal} is unambiguously signalled, as in prepositional phrases\is{prepositional!phrase}, in fact provide a more favourable environment for the preservation of the original \isi{case} forms. We have illustrated this with the data from \ili{Serbian} dialects\is{dialect} where the original \isi{genitive} \isi{case} forms alternate with the innovative \isi{accusative} forms. We analysed three \isi{construction} types: prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction}, adnominal constructions\is{adnominal construction}, and constructions\is{construction} with a \isi{quantifier}. In all the dialects\is{dialect} of the sample, prepositional constructions\is{prepositional!construction} preserved the original \isi{case} forms better than the other two. The three constructions\is{construction} vary with respect to the strength of connection between their elements. While the use of a \isi{noun} (such as `cup' or `top') or a \isi{quantifier} does not necessarily entail the subsequent use of a \isi{noun}, a \isi{preposition} obligatorily governs a noun phrase\is{noun!phrase}, marked with the \isi{genitive} in our case. Thus, the established association of a form with its apparent context turns out to be a key factor which determines speakers' choices and contributes to the preservation of the original \isi{case} forms.

Second, higher pragmatic salience\is{pragmatic!salience}, rather than triggering \isi{case} distinctions, as has been widely observed cross-linguistically, in fact leads to the opposite result in the \ili{Bulgarian} border-region dialects\is{dialect}. These dialects\is{dialect} retain at most two cases\is{case}, \isi{nominative} and \isi{accusative}. The \isi{accusative} is normally used for non-subjects\is{subject!non-subject}, but this is not always so: they may also take the \isi{nominative}, making them morphologically indistinguishable from subjects\is{subject} and thus effectively uninflected. This \isi{variation} is conditioned by \isi{information structure}: a \isi{subject} (\isi{nominative}) vs. non-subject\is{subject!non-subject} (\isi{accusative}) distinction is better maintained where background (given) information is being presented, while pragmatically salient\is{pragmatic!salience} parts of an utterance show a strong tendency to eliminate this distinction. Additional factors that play a role in limiting the use of the \isi{accusative} are the fact that only two classes of nouns\is{noun} are affected (inflection classes I and II\is{inflectional class}), and the overall marginalisation of \isi{case} distinctions on nouns\is{noun} in the context of the prestige standard language, which lacks \isi{nominal} \isi{case}.

The fine-grained analysis of the competing patterns of case marking\is{case!marking} provided here contributes to our understanding of factors that condition the loss of inflectional\is{inflection} case\is{case!loss of case}. In particular, different factors appear to be activated at different stages of the change. A synchronic\is{synchrony} cross-\isi{dialect} analysis therefore allows us to uncover the intricate detail of this historical process\il{Slavic!South|)}.
